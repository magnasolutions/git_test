### 1.2 Term, equity and the strike

| Term | Proposal | Basis / trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary term** | **3 years**, structured as **3 × consecutive 1-year Tawtheeq tenancies**, with a purchase option exercisable at the end of year 1, 2 or 3. | Mirrors Aldar's Ansam / Al Hadeel / Meera ladder (option at yr 1 / 2 / 3). Three years is where the accrued credit reaches the size of a typical expat mortgage deposit — see 1.2 note below |
| **5-year alternative** | Available at the developer's preference. | **Trade-off:** 5 years is the West Yas precedent (5 yrs, then transfer) and accrues far more equity — but it locks the developer's price for five years, doubles the counterparty exposure on both sides, and pushes the occupier's decision beyond a normal employment horizon. **3 years is proposed as the primary because it reaches a financeable deposit fastest with the least price-risk to the developer.** |
| **Equity accrual** | **100% of each rent payment credited against the purchase price**, tracked in a statement issued with each annual renewal. | Anchored on the two real datapoints: **West Yas — 100% of rent converted to equity at year 5 (~AED 1.1M on 220k/yr)**; **Ansam / Al Hadeel / Meera — 16% / 19% / 22% of property value credited at years 1 / 2 / 3**. |
| **Fallback if 100% is refused** | **50% of rent credited**, in exchange for a correspondingly lower strike or a longer option window. | Stated openly so the negotiation has somewhere to go without reopening the rent. |

**Why 100% of rent over 3 years is the ask, and why it should not frighten the developer** `[ILLUSTRATIVE]`

On a **AED 2.0M** indicative value at **AED 150,000/yr**, 100% rent credit accrues **7.5% of value per
rent-year → 22.5% by end of year 3.** That lands almost exactly on **Aldar's own published year-3
figure of 22%** for Ansam / Al Hadeel / Meera. In other words, *the headline ask is not more generous
than what an Abu Dhabi developer has already put in market* — it is the same number reached by a
simpler route.

It also sizes the deal. The research notes expat mortgage/Ijara minimum down payments of **20%+**.
A 22.5% accrued credit is **engineered to equal the deposit**, which is what makes the year-3 exercise
actually financeable rather than aspirational.

> **Honest caveat on the two anchors.** They cannot be fully reconciled. Aldar's *apartment* scheme
> appears to credit **more than 100% of rent** over three years (22% of value exceeds three years of
> the quoted rents unless the units were very cheap), which suggests the undisclosed "rebate on
> exercising the option" is doing part of the work. The underlying prices were **never published** for
> either scheme, so the true effective rate of both precedents is unknown. Both should be treated as
> negotiating anchors, not as a benchmark either side can verify.

| Term | Proposal | Trade-offs — **for the developer to choose** |
|---|---|---|
| **Option strike (how the future price is fixed)** | Three mechanisms offered. No preference asserted. | **(a) Fixed at signing.** Maximum certainty for the occupier; simplest to document; a lender can underwrite it. Developer carries all upside risk in a rising market. **(b) Indexed** — fixed base escalated by a published index or a capped fixed % p.a. Splits the risk; but the index must be one both sides can verify, and the **unverified reported ADREC rent freeze of 2 June 2026** (see Part 3) may make any escalation mechanic sensitive. **(c) Open-market valuation at exercise**, by an agreed independent valuer. ### 1.4 What happens if things change

*Commercial intent only. **Whether each of these outcomes is achievable and enforceable as drafted is a legal question for Part 3.***

| Event | Proposed commercial outcome |
|---|---|
| **Tenant defaults on rent** | Handled as **rent arrears under the registered tenancy**, through the ordinary ADREC rental-dispute route — *not* as a purchase default. Equity credits suspend while in arrears; cure period agreed; option lapses only on termination of the tenancy. **This is the single most important protection in the structure** (Part 2(c)) |
| **Tenant exits early** | Tenancy ends at the next annual expiry with agreed notice. Accrued equity credits **lapse** (they are consideration for continuing, not a debt of the developer) — proposed openly so the developer is not exposed to a repayment claim |
| **Tenant declines to exercise** | Option simply expires. Occupier reverts to an ordinary tenant, or vacates. Accrued credits lapse; option fee forfeited. **No liability on either side** — this is an *option*, not an obligation to buy, and that asymmetry is deliberate |
| **Employment ends** | Proposed: a **notice-and-exit window** (e.g. 60–90 days) at any point, terminating the tenancy without penalty beyond loss of accrued credits. The employer allowance is the funding source; when it stops, forcing the arrangement to continue helps neither party |
| **Developer sells the asset** | Proposed: **the tenancy and the purchase option bind any successor in title**, with the developer required to disclose the option to any buyer and procure the buyer's acknowledgement. ⚠️ **Whether this is achievable, and whether the option can be noted or registered anywhere so it survives a sale, is an open legal question — see Part 3** |
| **Developer becomes insolvent** | ⚠️ **Unresolved.** What happens to accrued equity credits is a Part 3 question and, on the research available, **has no known answer** |

## PART 2 — WHY A TAWTHEEQ TENANCY PLUS A SEPARATE OPTION, NOT AN SPA INSTALMENT PLAN

Every villa and townhouse product currently marketed in Abu Dhabi that resembles "pay while you
live in it" is, on the research available, an **SPA instalment or post-handover payment plan** —
Bloom Living, Fay Alreeman, Reportage, Al Ghadeer Gardens. In all of them the occupier is a **buyer
from day one**: no landlord, no lease, no Tawtheeq, no rent credited to anything.

This proposal is deliberately built the other way round: **an ordinary annual registered tenancy,
plus a separate purchase option sitting alongside it.** Three reasons, in ascending order of
importance.

**(a) It preserves ADREC rental-dispute protection for the occupier.**
The research is explicit that all Abu Dhabi residential leases must be Tawtheeq-registered
(ADREC, via TAMM) to be legally valid, and that **an unregistered tenancy contract is not legally
valid and cannot be enforced through the rental dispute committee.** Registration costs
**AED 900 one-off plus AED 50 per contract** — trivial money for a decisive difference in what forum
a dispute is heard in. Under an SPA there is no tenancy and therefore no rental-dispute route at all.

**(b) It produces an annual rent invoice an employer can pay directly — which is what makes the
whole thing fundable.**
This is the commercial keystone. Employer housing allowances in the UAE are typically paid **against
a registered tenancy contract with the employee named as tenant** — HR wants a Tawtheeq-registered
lease, an annual rent invoice, and a landlord to remit to. Under an SPA, the payment is a **property
purchase instalment**: the company would be funding an employee's personal capital accumulation,
which most employers cannot do from a housing-allowance line, and which may carry benefit-in-kind
and end-of-service consequences. The same objection applies to a bank Ijara — a finance lease with
the bank is not an ADREC-registered residential tenancy, so there is no rent for an employer to pay.

**The blunt version for the developer: an SPA-shaped product cannot reach the salaried tenant-buyer
whose housing allowance is the money. A Tawtheeq-shaped product can. That is the market this
structure opens, and it is the reason to build it.**

**(c) It keeps the occupier a tenant rather than a buyer-in-default if payments stop.**
Under an SPA, a missed instalment is a **purchase default**, exposing the deposit and every
instalment already paid. Under a tenancy, a missed payment is **rent arrears**, handled through the
rental-dispute committee with the protections that attach to it. A tenant-buyer will accept the loss
of accrued credits on exit — as proposed in 1.4. They will not accept the risk of being a
buyer-in-default on a six-figure sum in an arrangement funded by an employer allowance that may
stop with the job. **De-risking the downside for the occupier is what makes the product sellable at
volume.**

**Why annual, and why re-registered each year:** because that is the shape of an ordinary Abu Dhabi
tenancy, which is the shape the employer's finance function already knows how to pay. The purchase
option spans the full term and sits *outside* the annual lease; the lease renews under it.

> ⚠️ **Every proposition in this Part is a commercial rationale, not a legal opinion.** Whether the
> structure actually achieves (a), (b) or (c) as a matter of Abu Dhabi law and ADREC practice
> **must be confirmed by a UAE real-estate lawyer**. The research established the Tawtheeq
> *registration requirement* and the *consequence of non-registration*; it established **nothing**
> about how a lease-plus-option is treated. Note also that the research could **not obtain contract
> wording for a single Abu Dhabi rent-to-own scheme ever offered** — including whether West Yas or
> Ansam issued a Tawtheeq at all. **There is no known worked precedent for this structure.**

## PART 3 — OPEN LEGAL QUESTIONS A LAWYER MUST CLOSE ⚠️

**This section is not a formality. NONE of the questions below was verifiable in the underlying
research, because every regulator, developer and portal source was blocked (HTTP 403). This list is
NOT exhaustive. No item below should be treated as settled, and nothing in Parts 1–2 should be
relied on until a UAE-qualified real-estate lawyer has closed these — and identified the ones that
are missing.**

1. **Recharacterisation risk.** Could a lease-plus-option, with rent credited to a purchase price,
   be **recharacterised as a deferred sale or an off-plan sale** rather than a tenancy? If so, does
   that trigger **escrow-account requirements**, developer-registration obligations, or off-plan
   sale-registration requirements? *This is the question that determines whether the structure works
   at all.*
2. **Do the accrued equity credits create a registrable or protectable interest** in the property?
   If not, what — if anything — protects the occupier's accrued credit? If yes, where is it noted,
   and at what cost?
3. **Can the purchase option be made to bind a successor in title**, so it survives the developer
   selling the asset (§1.4)? Is there any mechanism to note or register it?
4. **How does a multi-year purchase option interact with annual ADREC/Tawtheeq registration?**
   Can a single option validly span three consecutive one-year registered tenancies? Does anything
   in the Tawtheeq contract form accommodate — or conflict with — an attached option?
5. **Rent-increase caps and the reported freeze.** The research records — **from a single brokerage
   blog, UNVERIFIED and not confirmed on adrec.gov.ae** — a temporary **ADREC freeze on all rent
   increases across Abu Dhabi effective 2 June 2026**, halting the standard 5% annual increase.
   Is it real? Is it still in force? Does it constrain the fixed-rent and indexed-strike mechanics
   in §1.2? Does any cap apply to renewal of the annual tenancies?
6. **Does the arrangement engage mortgage, finance or credit regulation?** Deferring a purchase price
   and crediting payments against it may look like the provision of finance. Does the developer need
   a licence or authorisation it does not have?
7. **VAT and fee treatment of the equity credit.** Is a rent payment that is simultaneously an equity
   credit still exempt residential rent? Does the credit change the VAT treatment of either leg? What
   is the treatment of the option fee? How is the 2% ADM/DARI transfer fee assessed — on the gross
   price or net of credits — and is the reported 1% buyer / 1% seller split correct and mandatory?
8. **Developer insolvency.** What happens to accrued equity credits if the developer fails? Are they
   a provable claim, a secured interest, or worth nothing? **No answer was found.**
9. **Employment-linked exit and forfeiture.** Are the forfeiture provisions in §1.4 enforceable, or
   could they be struck down as penal? Conversely, is the notice-and-exit window compatible with a
   registered tenancy?
10. **Employer payment mechanics.** Does an employer paying rent directly to the landlord under a
    tenancy that also carries a purchase option create any issue for the employer, the employee, or
    the landlord? *(The employer-side policy questions — allowance eligibility, benefit-in-kind,
    end-of-service gratuity — sit with HR, separately, and were also unresolved in the research.)*
11. **Whether any of this has been done before in Abu Dhabi.** The two precedents cited throughout
    this document — **West Yas (2019)** and **Ansam / Al Hadeel / Meera (2019)** — are both
    **unverified, undocumented as to contract type, and probably closed.** No contract wording was
    obtainable for either. **It is unknown whether either was a Tawtheeq tenancy or an SPA.** If they
    were SPAs, the central premise of Part 2 is untested in this market.

**Bottom line: the commercial logic in Parts 1–2 is coherent and, on the research available, fills a
real gap in the Abu Dhabi market. Its legal viability is entirely unestablished. Treat this document
as the agenda for a first meeting with a lawyer and a developer — in that order.**
