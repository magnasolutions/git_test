### 0.3 — On the arithmetic available, the obvious version of this product does not close

Independent review rebuilt the economics from the campaign's own inputs (model unit: value
**AED 2.2M**, rent **AED 150,000/yr**, option fee **AED 110,000** credited on exercise) and set the
two constraints against each other — the credit rate a developer can afford while beating its best
live alternative, against the credit rate the occupier needs to reach a financeable deposit.

| Developer hurdle | Term | Developer can afford | Occupier needs | Gap (% of asset value) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6% | 3 yr | 39.6% | 73.3% | −5.8% |
| 6% | 5 yr | 35.3% | 44.0% | −2.2% |
| 8% | 3 yr | 42.1% | 73.3% | −5.1% |
| **8%** | **5 yr** | **38.5%** | **44.0%** | **−1.3%** ← marked as the closest miss |
| 10% | 3 yr | 44.6% | 73.3% | −4.4% |
| 10% | 5 yr | 41.9% | 44.0% | −0.4% |

**The regions never overlap, at any term from 3 to 5 years, at any hurdle from 6% to 10%.**

Three things must be said plainly rather than buried:

- **Three-year terms are dead.** The occupier would need a 73.3% credit rate. Nothing gets there.
- **At a 10% hurdle there is no combination at all** — the review found a 10% clearing sale beats
  every occupier-feasible version at every term from 3 to 10 years. *(The −0.4% figure in the 10%/5yr
  row above sits oddly against that conclusion; the review marks the 8%/5yr row as the closest miss.
  The tension is reproduced rather than resolved — the two lines use different comparators, and this
  should be checked against the developer's own numbers, not ours.)* **If your hurdle is near 10%,
  take the discount, recycle the capital, and stop reading.**
- **It gets worse, not better, once transaction costs are included** — see §1.2, note B.

**This document therefore does not ask for a credit rate. It asks the developer to set one, and
discloses the level below which the occupier cannot complete.** If those two numbers do not meet,
they do not meet, and that is worth establishing in one meeting rather than three.

**And the honest framing of what is being asked for**, since a director will reach it anyway:

| Structure | Nominal give-up | % of value | PV @ 8% *(rounded)* | PV as % of value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% clearing discount today | 220,000 | 10.0% | 220,000 | **10.0%** |
| 5 yr @ 44% credit + option fee *(min. occupier-feasible)* | 440,000 | 20.0% | ~300,000 | **13.6%** |
| 5 yr @ 50% credit + option fee | 485,000 | 22.0% | ~330,000 | **15.0%** |

**A rent credit against a strike is a discount.** It is a larger discount in present value than a
10% clearing discount, and it is delivered five years later. Any pitch claiming otherwise — that
this is "a pricing mechanism, not a giveaway", or that the developer "is not selling at a discount,
it is selling an option" — is wrong, and a commercial director will say so in the first minute.
The counter-argument is not that it isn't a discount. It is that on **genuinely stalled stock** the
comparator is not a clearing sale at 10% but a unit that is not moving at all — **which is a question
about your inventory, not ours.**

## PART 2 — WHY A TAWTHEEQ TENANCY PLUS A SEPARATE OPTION, RATHER THAN AN SPA INSTALMENT PLAN

Everything the research found currently marketed in the Abu Dhabi villa/townhouse segment is an
**SPA instalment or post-handover payment plan**. In those, the occupier is a **buyer from day one**:
no landlord, no lease, no Tawtheeq, no rent credited to anything.

This proposal is built the other way round — an ordinary registered tenancy, with a purchase option
alongside it. Three reasons, in ascending order of importance. **All three are reasoning, not
observation, and the third is qualified by the forum caveat above.**

**(a) It is intended to preserve rental-dispute protection for the occupier.**
`[UNVERIFIED — research states]` all Abu Dhabi residential leases must be Tawtheeq-registered
(ADREC, via TAMM) to be legally valid, and an unregistered tenancy contract cannot be enforced
through the rental dispute committee. **That the research states this does not make it settled**, and
it says nothing about how a lease carrying a purchase option is treated. Under an SPA there is no
tenancy and so no rental-dispute route at all — but **whether this structure secures one is
precisely what Part 3 asks.**

**(b) It is intended to produce an annual rent invoice an employer can pay directly.**
`[ASSUMPTION — no employer policy was surveyed; no HR department was asked; the research contains
nothing on employer housing-allowance practice.]` **The reasoning, offered as reasoning:** an
employer housing allowance is generally understood to be paid against a registered tenancy with the
employee named as tenant — a lease, an annual rent invoice, a landlord to remit to. Under an SPA the
payment would be a **property purchase instalment**, i.e. the employer funding an employee's capital
accumulation, which may raise benefit-in-kind and end-of-service questions. A bank Ijara raises the
same issue in different clothing — though **that is a characterisation of a regulated bank product
made without access to any bank's documentation, and it should not be relied on.**

**None of the above has been confirmed by any employer.** Rev. 1 stated this premise affirmatively
and in bold; that was not supportable. **See §0.1: this is the threshold question, and it is open.**

**(c) It is intended to keep the occupier a tenant rather than a buyer-in-default if payments stop.**
Under an SPA a missed instalment is a **purchase default**, exposing the deposit and every instalment
already paid. Under a tenancy a missed payment is **rent arrears**. An occupier will accept losing
accrued credits on exit (§1.4); they will not accept buyer-in-default exposure on a six-figure sum in
an arrangement funded by an allowance that may stop with the job. ⚠️ **But the forum caveat above
limits how much of this survives** — the protection may reach the rent and not the credit.

**One correction to how this has been pitched.** Rental-dispute protection is a benefit to the
**occupier**. It should not be presented to a developer as a benefit to the developer. Nor should
"zero exposure at each renewal": under a plain tenancy prepaid rent extinguishes as it is consumed,
whereas here a growing share becomes an accrued ledger the developer owes back — by year 5 a
400–500k obligation with unresolved forfeiture enforceability. **The exposure profile is worse than
an ordinary tenancy, not better,** and a developer will work that out.

**What is honestly true about the occupier**, and it is a statement about the occupier's constraint
rather than a benefit to the developer: *the allowance is why this tenant can pay AED 150,000/yr and
cannot pay a AED 500,000 deposit.* That is the whole of it. Annual-in-advance payment is not a credit
story; it is how a normal tenant pays.

## PART 3 — OPEN QUESTIONS A LAWYER MUST CLOSE ⚠️

**None of the questions below was verifiable in the underlying research, because every regulator,
developer and portal source was blocked (HTTP 403). This list is NOT exhaustive — and the lawyer is
asked specifically to identify the questions that are missing from it. Nothing in Parts 0–2 should be
relied on until these are closed.**

**Q0. Can an expatriate occupier take freehold title in each named community?** *(Asked first because
it sits upstream of everything else — you can win every argument below and still hold a worthless
option. See §0.2. Only Al Reef is confirmed; the rest are unestablished.)*

**Q1. Recharacterisation.** Could a lease-plus-option, with rent credited against a purchase price,
be treated as a **deferred or off-plan sale** rather than a tenancy? If so, does that trigger
**escrow-account requirements**, developer-registration or off-plan sale-registration obligations?
*Which of the terms in §1.2 raise that risk most, and by how much?* **If this comes back the wrong
way there is no Tawtheeq, no dispute route, and no rent for an employer to pay — the entire premise
collapses at once.**

**Q2. Do the accrued credits create any registrable or protectable interest?** If not, what protects
them? If yes, where is it noted and at what cost? **Are they property or a bare contractual
expectation** — the question underlying death, assignment, forfeiture and insolvency alike?

**Q3. Forum.** The option and the equity ledger sit outside the registered tenancy. **Which body
hears a dispute about the accrued credit, the strike, or a refusal to complete?** Does the
rental-dispute route reach any of it, or only the rent?

**Q4. Can the option be made to bind a successor in title** so it survives a sale of the asset? Is
there any mechanism to note or register it?

**Q5. Registration shape.** Can a **five-year residential tenancy** be registered on these terms? If
not, can a single option validly span five successive annual registrations? Can a side agreement
**pre-fix the rent and the renewal** of a future registered tenancy, and **which document governs on
conflict** — the registered contract or the annexe?

**Q6. Break rights and forfeiture.** Can a unilateral occupier break right (§1.4, employment ending)
be written into a registered tenancy form? Are the forfeiture provisions enforceable, or could they
be struck down as penal — or reversed into restitution if the payments are recharacterised?

**Q7. Rent caps — and which leg they attach to.** The research records, **from a single brokerage
blog, UNVERIFIED and not confirmed on adrec.gov.ae**, a temporary ADREC freeze on all rent increases
across Abu Dhabi effective 2 June 2026. Is it real and still in force? Then the sharper questions:
**is a rent that is simultaneously an equity credit treated as rent for cap purposes at all?** And in
reverse — **can an occupier validly pre-agree a fixed five-year rent that forgoes a benefit a cap
regime would otherwise confer at renewal?**

**Q8. Does the arrangement engage mortgage, finance or credit regulation?** Deferring a purchase
price and crediting payments against it may look like providing finance. Does the developer need an
authorisation it does not hold?

**Q9. VAT and fee treatment.** Is a rent payment that is simultaneously an equity credit still exempt
residential rent? What is the treatment of the option fee? Is the 2% transfer fee assessed on gross
price or net of credits, and is the reported 1%/1% split correct and mandatory?

**Q10. Custody of the credits.** Is third-party custody achievable — and does proposing it increase
the recharacterisation risk in Q1 by importing off-plan sale machinery? *(§1.2 raises this as a

**Q11. Insolvency, both sides.** If the developer fails, are the credits a provable claim, a secured
interest, or worth nothing? If the occupier fails, do the credits fall into the estate and what can a
trustee do with the option? **No answer was found to either.**

**Q12. Employer payment mechanics.** Does an employer paying rent directly to a landlord under a
tenancy carrying a purchase option create any issue for the employer, the employee or the landlord?
*(Separate from §0.1, which asks the prior question: whether any employer will do it at all.)*

**Q13. Has any of this been done here before?** The two precedents cited throughout — **West Yas
(2019)** and **Ansam / Al Hadeel / Meera (2019)** — are unverified, probably closed, and **undocumented
as to contract type. It is unknown whether either issued a Tawtheeq or was an SPA.**

## WHAT IS ACTUALLY BEING ASKED FOR

Not a signature, and not a unit. The proposer is one prospective tenant-buyer, and a single-unit deal
is a bespoke transaction whose legal and treasury cost may exceed its margin — **that is a fair
objection and it is conceded.**

Three questions, all answerable in one meeting:

1. **Do you hold stock that is genuinely stalled** — not letting, not selling — where the comparator
   is a void rather than a clearing sale? If not, §0.3 says stop here.
2. **What credit rate and what tranche size would make a structure like this worth building?** The
   answer is taken as information, not negotiated. If the number is "none", that is a useful answer
   and it closes the question.
3. **Would you look at it again if the proposer came back with (i) a written employer confirmation,
   (ii) one lender's written position that accrued rent credit counts as the borrower's equity, and
   (iii) a named group of occupiers rather than one?** Those three are the proposer's cost to obtain,
   not yours.

**The honest closing position.** A gap in *supply* is documented — no Abu Dhabi villa or townhouse
rent-to-own at this price point was found to exist. **Nothing here evidences unmet *demand*, and this
document does not claim to.** The commercial logic in Parts 0–1 does not currently close on the
arithmetic available; its legal viability is entirely unestablished; and the demand behind it is one
person. **Treat this as the agenda for a first conversation with a lawyer and a lender — in that
order, and both before a developer commits anything.**
